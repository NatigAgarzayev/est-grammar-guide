Peatükk 1 · Sissejuhatus

Introduction to Estonian

What kind of language Estonian is, how its alphabet works, and how to read words aloud before you know any grammar at all.

About the Estonian language

Estonian (eesti keel) is spoken by about 1.1 million people, mainly in Estonia. It is not related to Russian, German, or the other Indo-European languages around it. Instead it belongs to the Finno-Ugric branch of the Uralic family, making Finnish its closest major relative and Hungarian a distant cousin.

For a learner, this family background explains most of what makes Estonian feel unusual:

FeatureWhat it means for you
No grammatical gendertema means both he and she — one word, no guessing.
No articlesThere is no a or the. Raamat is "a book" or "the book" depending on context.
14 grammatical casesWord endings do the work that English prepositions do: majas = "in the house".
No future tenseThe present tense covers the future: Homme lähen tööle = "Tomorrow I('ll) go to work".
Agglutinative endingsMeanings stack onto word stems piece by piece, so words can get long but stay logical.
Vihje · Tip

Cases sound intimidating, but most of them behave like glued-on prepositions. If you can learn "in", "on", and "with" in English, you can learn -s, -l, and -ga in Estonian.

The alphabet

Estonian uses the Latin alphabet with a few extra letters: õ, ä, ö, ü, plus š and ž in loanwords. The letters c, q, w, x, y appear only in foreign names. Spelling is almost perfectly phonetic — once you know the sounds, you can read any word aloud.

Consonant notes

Consonants are close to English with a few quirks: r is rolled, s is always voiceless (never a "z" sound), and h is light. The letters b, d, g are pronounced softly, almost like weak p, t, k. š ("sh") and ž ("zh") appear only in loanwords like šokolaad (chocolate) and žest (gesture).

Pane tähele · Note

The vowel õ is the most famously difficult Estonian sound, and even neighbouring Finnish does not have it. Don't worry about perfecting it early — Estonians will understand you, and your ear will tune in with exposure.

Pronunciation guide

Two rules carry you a long way. First, stress is always on the first syllable of a word (rare exceptions like aitäh and some loanwords). Second, every letter is pronounced — there are no silent letters.

Estonian also distinguishes three degrees of length in vowels and consonants: short, long, and overlong. Double letters in writing mark long sounds; overlong length is not written but is heard.

DegreeWordMeaning
Shortlinalinen, sheet
Longlinnaof the city (genitive)
Overlonglinnainto the city (illative)
Vihje · Tip

Length can change a word's grammatical meaning, as in linna above — the spelling is identical and only the spoken length differs. As a beginner, simply hold double letters noticeably longer than single ones and let the rest come with listening practice.

Nouns & cases — a gentle introduction

Estonian nouns have no gender and no articles, but they change their endings depending on their role in the sentence. Every noun has three basic forms that you should learn together, like a verb's principal parts:

FormQuestionExampleUsed for
nimetav (nominative)kes? mis?raamatthe dictionary form; the subject of a sentence
omastav (genitive)kelle? mille?raamatupossession ("of the book"); the stem most other cases are built on
osastav (partitive)keda? mida?raamatutpartial or indefinite objects, amounts, after numbers

A few more nouns in all three forms — notice that the changes are not always predictable, which is exactly why the forms are memorised as a set:

NimetavOmastavOsastavMeaning

sõbersõbrasõprafriend

tüdruktüdrukutüdrukutgirl
Vihje · Tip

Whenever you learn a new noun, learn it as a triple: raamat – raamatu – raamatut. Almost all of the 14 cases are formed by adding a simple ending to the omastav form, so these three forms unlock the whole system.

Personal pronouns

Each pronoun has a long and a short form. The short forms are used in everyday neutral speech; the long forms add emphasis or contrast.

Teie doubles as the polite singular "you", like French vous. Use it with strangers, officials, and older people until invited to switch to sina. And since Estonian has no gender, tema/ta covers both "he" and "she".

The verb olema — to be

Olema is the most frequent verb in Estonian. Its present tense is irregular but short, and the third person uses the same form in singular and plural.

PersonAffirmativeNegativeEnglish

saoledei ole / poleyou are / are not
taonei ole / polehe/she is / is not
meolemeei ole / polewe are / are not

Vihje · Tip

Estonian has no verb "to have". Possession is expressed with olema and the owner in the -l case: Mul on raamat — literally "On me is a book" = "I have a book".

The 14 grammatical cases — overview

Here is the complete system with raamat (book). The first three you already know; cases 4–9 are the locative cases, which come in two neat triplets (movement into → being in → movement out of).

#CaseEnglish nameQuestionExampleMeaning
1nimetavnominativekes? mis?raamatbook
2omastavgenitivekelle? mille?raamatuof the book
3osastavpartitivekeda? mida?raamatut(some) book
4sisseütlevillativekuhu?raamatusseinto the book
5seesütlevinessivekus?raamatusin the book
6seestütlevelativekust?raamatustout of / about the book
7alaleütlevallativekellele? millele?raamatuleonto the book
8alalütlevadessivekellel? millel?raamatulon the book
9alaltütlevablativekellelt? millelt?raamatultoff the book
10saavtranslativekelleks? milleks?raamatuks(turning) into a book
11rajavterminativekelleni? milleni?raamatuniup to the book
12olevessivekellena? millena?raamatunaas a book
13ilmaütlevabessivekelleta? milleta?raamatutawithout the book
14kaasaütlevcomitativekellega? millega?raamatugawith the book
Vihje · Tip

Notice that cases 4–14 are all built on the genitive stem raamatu- plus a regular ending. The endings themselves (-sse, -s, -st, -le, -l, -lt, -ks, -ni, -na, -ta, -ga) never change from noun to noun — only the stem does.

Pane tähele · Note

The inner triplet (-sse / -s / -st) is for enclosed spaces, and the outer triplet (-le / -l / -lt) for surfaces — compare majas "in the house" with laual "on the table". Many nouns also have a short illative: majja = majasse.

Present tense verbs

Regular present-tense endings are added to the verb stem and are the same for every verb in the language:

PersonEndingelama (to live)õppima (to study)
ma-nelanõpin
sa-deladõpid
ta-belabõpib
me-meelameõpime
te-teelateõpite
nad-vadelavadõpivad
Pane tähele · Note

Did you spot õppima → õpin? Many Estonian words alternate between a "strong" and a "weak" stem (pp → p, kk → k, b → v...). This is called consonant gradation and it affects nouns too (sõber → sõbra). Learn each verb's ma-form alongside its infinitive.

Näitelaused · Example sentences

Ma elan Tartus ja töötan kodus.

I live in Tartu and work from home.

Me õpime eesti keelt.

We are studying Estonian.

Ta räägib kolme keelt.

He/She speaks three languages.

Negation

Negation is refreshingly simple: put ei before the bare present stem. The verb form is identical for every person — no endings at all.

AffirmativeNegativeEnglish
ma elanma ei elaI don't live
sa eladsa ei elayou don't live
ta elabta ei elahe/she doesn't live
me elameme ei elawe don't live
te elatete ei elayou don't live
nad elavadnad ei elathey don't live

Näitelaused · Example sentences

Ma ei tea.

I don't know.

Ta ei räägi vene keelt.

He/She doesn't speak Russian.

Me ei lähe homme tööle.

We are not going to work tomorrow.

Vihje · Tip

Unlike English, there is no "do" helper to juggle — ei + stem covers everything. The negative of olema has a handy fused form: ei ole and pole are fully interchangeable.

Adjective agreement

An adjective standing before a noun agrees with it in case and number — both words take the ending together:

CasePhraseMeaning
nimetavsuur majaa big house
omastavsuure majaof the big house
osastavsuurt maja(a) big house (object)
seesütlevsuures majasin the big house
alalütlevsuurel maalin the big country(side)
kaasaütlevsuure majagawith the big house
Vihje · Tip

There is one famous exception: in the last four cases (rajav, olev, ilmaütlev, kaasaütlev — -ni, -na, -ta, -ga), the adjective stays in the genitive while only the noun takes the ending: suure majani, suure majata, suure majaga.

Basic sentence structure

The neutral order is Subject – Verb – Object, just like English. But because cases mark each word's role, the order can flex for emphasis without losing meaning:

SentenceEmphasis
Mari loeb raamatut.neutral: Mari is reading a book
Raamatut loeb Mari.it's Mari (not someone else) who reads the book
Täna loeb Mari raamatut.today (verb moves to keep second position)
Pane tähele · Note

The third example shows the verb-second tendency: when a time or place word opens the sentence, the verb still comes second and the subject slides after it. There is much more on this in the B1 chapter.

Näitelaused · Example sentences

Ma joon hommikul kohvi.

I drink coffee in the morning.

Õhtul vaatame me filmi.

In the evening we watch a film.

Eesti keelt õpin ma iga päev.

Estonian I study every day. (emphasis on Estonian)

Past & future tenses

Estonian has three past tenses. The workhorse is the simple past (lihtminevik), usually marked with -si-:

Personõppima → pastolema → past
maõppisinolin
saõppisidolid
taõppisoli
meõppisimeolime
teõppisiteolite
nadõppisidolid

The two compound pasts are built with olema + the -nud participle, exactly parallel to English "have done / had done":

TenseFormExampleTranslation
lihtminevik (simple past)õppisinMa õppisin eile.I studied yesterday.
täisminevik (perfect)olen õppinudMa olen eesti keelt õppinud kaks aastat.I have studied Estonian for two years.
enneminevik (pluperfect)olin õppinudMa olin juba õppinud, kui ta saabus.I had already studied when he arrived.
Pane tähele · Note

The negative past uses ei + the -nud participle for all persons: ma ei õppinud, nad ei läinud. A few common verbs have irregular pasts — most importantly minema (to go) → läksin, läinud.

The future — without a future tense

Estonian has no morphological future. The present tense plus context does the job, with a couple of helper constructions:

StrategyExampleTranslation
present + time wordHomme lähen ma tööle.Tomorrow I'll go to work.
hakkama + ma-infinitiveMa hakkan eesti keelt õppima.I'm going to start learning Estonian.
saama (will get/become)Kõik saab korda.Everything will be fine.

Postpositions

Where English puts prepositions before a noun, Estonian usually puts postpositions after it, with the noun in the genitive: laua peal = "on the table" (literally "table's on-top"). Like the locative cases, they come in triplets:

MeaningWhere? (static)Where to?Where from?
on top ofpealpealepealt
underallallaalt
behindtagatahatagant
in front ofeesetteeest
next tokõrvalkõrvalekõrvalt
at / byjuuresjuurdejuurest

Näitelaused · Example sentences

Raamat on laua peal.

The book is on the table.

Pane raamat laua peale.

Put the book onto the table.

Võta raamat laua pealt.

Take the book off the table.

Buss peatub maja ees.

The bus stops in front of the house.

Vihje · Tip

A handful of words work as prepositions instead, often pairing with a specific case: ilma autota ("without a car", abessive), koos sõbraga ("together with a friend", comitative). The triplet logic — static / towards / away — stays the same everywhere.

Conditional mood

The conditional ("would") is marked with -ksi- and is also the standard tool for polite requests:

Personelama → conditionalolema → conditional

saelaksidoleksid

meelaksimeoleksime

Pane tähele · Note

In casual speech, the personal endings often drop and the bare -ks form serves every person: ma elaks, me läheks. The past conditional ("would have") combines both moods: ma oleksin õppinud — "I would have studied".

Näitelaused · Example sentences

Ma tahaksin kohvi, palun.

I would like a coffee, please.

Kas sa aitaksid mind?

Would you help me?

Kui mul oleks rohkem aega, õpiksin iga päev.

If I had more time, I would study every day.

Yes/no questions begin with the particle kas — the rest of the sentence keeps normal word order. Content questions begin with a question word:

Vihje · Tip

Notice the three-way "where": kus (location), kuhu (direction towards), kust (direction from) — the same triplet logic as the locative cases and postpositions. Negative-leaning questions use ega: Ega sa ei tea, kus ta on? — "You don't happen to know where he is?"

Word order rules

Estonian word order is flexible but not random. The strongest rule is the verb-second (V2) tendency: in a neutral declarative sentence, the finite verb wants to be the second element, whatever comes first.

First elementSentenceTranslation
subjectMa käisin eile poes.I went to the shop yesterday.
time wordEile käisin ma poes.Yesterday I went to the shop.
place wordPoes käisin ma eile.It was the shop I went to yesterday.
Pane tähele · Note

Good news for anyone who has fought with German: in Estonian subordinate clauses, the verb does not move to the end. Ma tean, et ta elab Tartus — "I know that he lives in Tartu" — keeps plain SVO order inside the et-clause.

Näitelaused · Example sentences

Täna õhtul vaatame me filmi.

This evening we'll watch a film.

Tartus elab palju tudengeid.

Many students live in Tartu.

Suvel sõidame me Saaremaale.

In summer we'll travel to Saaremaa.

Upper-Intermediate

The subtler machinery: how object case expresses aspect, joining clauses, the four participles, reporting what others said, and idioms that make you sound local.

Verb aspect — total vs partial objects

Estonian has no continuous tense and no perfective prefix system. Instead, the case of the object tells you whether an action is complete. This is one of the language's most elegant — and most examined — features.

Object caseSignalsExampleTranslation
osastav (partial object)ongoing, incomplete, or partial actionMa lugesin raamatut.I was reading the book.
omastav (total object, sg)completed action affecting the whole objectMa lugesin raamatu läbi.I read the book from cover to cover.
nimetav (total object, pl)completed, whole, pluralMa sõin õunad ära.I ate (all) the apples.

Completion is often reinforced by a verbal particle, a small adverb that works much like English "up" or "through":

Particle verbMeaning
ära söömato eat up
läbi lugemato read through
valmis kirjutamato finish writing
üles leidmato find, track down
ära õppimato learn thoroughly, master

Näitelaused · Example sentences

Ma sõin suppi.

I was eating (some) soup.

Ma sõin supi ära.

I ate the soup up — finished it.

Ta kirjutas kirja terve õhtu.

He was writing the letter all evening.

Ta kirjutas kirja valmis.

He finished writing the letter.

Vihje · Tip

When in doubt, the partitive is the safer default — verbs of emotion, perception, and ongoing activity (armastama, nägema, ootama...) almost always take a partitive object: Ma armastan sind, never sinu.

Complex sentence construction

Clauses join with conjunctions, and a comma is written before the subordinate clause — Estonian comma rules are strict and consistent:

ConjunctionEnglishExampleTranslation
etthatMa arvan, et eesti keel on ilus.I think that Estonian is beautiful.
sestbecauseJään koju, sest sajab vihma.I'll stay home because it's raining.
kuiif / whenKui vihma sajab, jään ma koju.If it rains, I'll stay home.
kuigialthoughKuigi on külm, lähen ma välja.Although it's cold, I'll go out.
kunasince, asKuna ma õpin, ei saa ma tulla.Since I'm studying, I can't come.
nii etso thatRäägi valjemini, nii et kõik kuulevad.Speak louder, so that everyone hears.

Relative clauses use kes (for people) and mis (for things), and they decline like any other pronoun:

Näitelaused · Example sentences

Mees, kes seisab ukse juures, on mu õpetaja.

The man who is standing by the door is my teacher.

Raamat, mille ma eile ostsin, on väga huvitav.

See on linn, kus ma sündisin.

This is the city where I was born.

Pane tähele · Note

Unlike English, the comma before et, kes, mis, kui and friends is mandatory, not stylistic. Estonian readers genuinely notice when it's missing.

Participles

Estonian has four participles — two voices times two tenses. Shown here with lugema (to read):

ParticipleEndingFormExample phraseTranslation
present active-vlugevlugev lapsa reading child
present passive-tavloetavkergesti loetav raamatan easily readable book
past active-nudlugenudpalju lugenud inimenea well-read person

You already know two of these as verb forms: -nud builds the perfect tenses (olen lugenud), and -tud builds the passive (Raamat on läbi loetud — "The book has been read through").

Näitelaused · Example sentences

Magav kass lamab diivanil.

The sleeping cat is lying on the sofa.

Väsinud õpetaja läks koju.

The tired teacher went home.

See on kõige loetavam eesti romaan.

This is the most-read Estonian novel.

Reported speech

Everyday reported speech uses an et-clause, and — pleasantly — the tense usually stays the same as in the original, with no backshifting:

Direct speechReported speechTranslation
„Ma tulen homme."Ta ütles, et ta tuleb homme.He said (that) he's coming tomorrow.
„Ma olen väsinud."Ta ütles, et ta on väsinud.She said (that) she's tired.
„Ma õppisin eile."Ta ütles, et ta õppis eile.He said (that) he studied yesterday.

Estonian also has something rarer: a dedicated quotative mood (kaudne kõneviis) with the ending -vat, used for hearsay — information you haven't verified yourself:

Näitelaused · Example sentences

Ta tulevat homme.

He is said to be coming tomorrow. / Apparently he's coming tomorrow.

Ta olevat haige.

He is said to be ill.

Seal elavat palju linde.

Many birds supposedly live there.

Vihje · Tip

The quotative is a built-in "allegedly". Newspapers and gossip alike use it to signal second-hand information without spelling out "people say that...". Recognising it is essential at B2; producing it makes you sound impressively fluent.
